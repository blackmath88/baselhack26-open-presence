# How we got here

31 October 2026 · Editorial provenance

## 1. Starting question

Achim asked for research into the historical problem solved by PDF: the pain point, technical response, value to users and business value. The intended medium was a retro 1990s/2000s HTML scrolly, following his [Scrollies practice](https://github.com/blackmath88/scrollies), potentially self-contained.

The broader project already asked: “PDF helped us preserve a document when it travelled. What should we preserve when an answer travels through AI?” That is an optional framing for Open Presence, not proof that PDF and ATProto solve equivalent problems.

## 2. Research brief

We drafted a Perplexity Research prompt with these requirements:

1. Reconstruct late-1980s/early-1990s document exchange, including alternatives that already worked.
2. Explain Camelot, PostScript and PDF mechanisms; separate original capabilities from later additions.
3. Investigate difficult early adoption, reader pricing, free distribution and standards.
4. Separate user value, organisational value, Adobe value capture and ecosystem opportunities.
5. Document limitations: semantics, accessibility, editing, versions, context and preservation.
6. Critically test the analogy to contribution records; use official ATProto documentation.
7. Supply an 8–10-scene handoff, causal visual transformations and small experiments.
8. Prefer primary and contemporary sources, attribute customer savings and label interpretation.
9. Produce research, not HTML; target a standalone offline HTML experience later.

The supplied dossier is preserved in [research/perplexity-report.md](research/perplexity-report.md). Original upload label: `Pasted text(20261031-094845).txt`. The upload contained a tool-output JSON wrapper; only its text-valued `output` was extracted. Editorial warnings were added above the otherwise preserved report.

It is a supplied Perplexity research result, not a separately verified historical account. Its own statement about authenticated GitHub reads belongs to that report and is not evidence that its cited historical sources were checked here.

## 3. What we took from it

The strongest proposed spine: **sending the file was not necessarily sending the document**. Source applications, versions, fonts and rendering environments could remain dependencies.

The report adds an essential adoption/business question: a portable object needs a usable receiving environment. We should investigate how free receiving increased the value of paid creation and workflows rather than treating technical invention as automatic adoption.

These are editorial synthesis and research directions. Historical dates, prices and quantitative customer claims retain their verification requirements.

## 4. Editorial compression

The dossier proposed ten scenes. We proposed six public-facing acts:

1. It breaks.
2. Send the paper.
3. Pack the page.
4. Who can open it?
5. Who pays—and why?
6. What survived?

Use states inside those acts for the technical detail. Do not turn every animation into a new scene. Give business value a full act. Keep the ATProto/project bridge short and detachable.

Proposed closing: **“The page arrived intact. What should arrive with an answer?”**

A synthetic service manual can persist across the story. It must be labelled an illustrative composite, never a real FPL or Adobe document.

## 7. Evidence boundaries

- Preserve the original report; correct claims in the ledger and later story rather than silently editing the historical import.
- Broken inline citations and bibliography numbering require reconciliation.
- Vendor-reported savings are not independent measurement.
- PDF preserves selected representation; it does not establish truth or complete context.
- ATProto record authentication/portability does not guarantee source availability, discovery, moderation or community.
- The current project remains useful without the Adobe analogy.
